# Automated AI review posting, and how the platforms and the UK regulator catch it

Date: 2026-10-31. Status: researched, public sources only; nothing posted anywhere, nothing pointed at any platform.

## What it is

The subject came from an Ask HN post of 24 September 2026 (platevoltage, 51 comments): a freelance client, not a programmer, is building a restaurant app with Lovable and plans to bolt on automated marketing posts from Higgsfield and "agents" that post reviews for the restaurants that use it. The thread gave no mechanism, only the intent, and nobody in it discussed how review platforms catch such posting. That is the gap this note fills. The thing itself is old (review brokers, review rings, bought five-stars); what is new in 2026 is that the person commissioning it does not think of it as fraud, because an app builder and a video generator make it look like a feature. Last week's Higgsfield note covered the video half. This one covers the review half: what the law now calls it, what the platforms say they do about it, and what the numbers look like.

## What it costs

Nothing is bought here and I priced no broker. The tools in the client's plan are ordinary subscriptions (Lovable for the app, Higgsfield from about $15 a month for the clips, covered in `intel/higgsfield-ai-video-marketing-kits.md`), which is the point: the marginal cost of a fake review has gone from a few pounds a piece through a broker to the cost of an API call. The cost that matters sits on the other side. In the UK, since 6 April 2025, submitting, commissioning, publishing or facilitating fake reviews is a banned practice under paragraph 13 of Schedule 20 to the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, automatically unfair with no harm test, and the CMA can enforce it directly rather than through the courts. The CMA's first fine under its new consumer powers, £473,000 to Euro Car Parks in February 2026, was for not answering an information notice, not for reviews, but it shows the direct route is live. The headline fining power for a substantive breach is up to 10% of worldwide turnover; I have that from the Act's direct-enforcement regime as reported, not re-read tonight.

## How it works

The parts a defender needs.

- **Three places to be caught, by law.** CMA208 (the fake reviews guidance, 4 April 2025) draws the supply chain as three prohibited acts: submitting or commissioning banned reviews, publishing reviews or review information in a misleading way, and offering services that procure or facilitate either. Its own examples name the automated case twice: "buying reviews which look like they have been written by individual consumers but have in fact been generated by software applications (such as bots)" is commissioning, and selling such reviews is procurement. Chapter 7 goes further: a service whose value is "expertise in bypassing the platform or website's fake review detection measures" is itself banned, as is setting up accounts "in a way that avoids or reduces the risk of detection". So the agent that posts, the app that sells the agent, and the restaurant that switches it on are each inside the ban.
- **A positive duty on whoever publishes.** Chapter 8 puts a "positive obligation" on every publisher of reviews, from Google and Yelp to a restaurant's own site, to have a written policy, assess the risk, detect, investigate, sanction, and re-assess regularly. A restaurant app that displays reviews it generated is a publisher failing that duty as well as a commissioner.
- **The platforms screen before publication, at the account level, not the sentence level.** This is the part the HN thread missed. Amazon says it screens every submitted review with models before it is published, using the seller's ad spend, the reviewer's history, abuse reports and incentive anomalies, and maps relationships between accounts with graph neural networks; it blocked more than 250 million suspected fake reviews in 2023. Yelp's recommendation software weighs "hundreds of signals of quality, reliability, and user activity" and its 2025 report adds filters for reviews "lacking sufficient detail" and for undisclosed business ties. Google's Maps policy bans fake engagement outright and names the shapes: paid reviews, "content posted from multiple accounts by or at the request of one person", and posting through emulators, tampered devices or modified operating systems to mimic genuine engagement; enforcement runs from suspending account privileges to termination, decided on "the content itself, account information, user actions" and whether a pattern exists.
- **AI text is a policy breach on its own at Yelp, and a weak signal everywhere.** Yelp's policy is that reviews must not be "drafted or revised by third-party AI tools", and it filtered nearly 500,000 suspected AI-generated reviews in 2025 on that basis. Google's policy page has no AI clause at all. The research position, which I think is right, is that AI prose alone does not establish a fake: genuine customers polish real experiences with the same tools, so the text classifier is a prior, and the account graph, the timing and the purchase or visit record are the evidence.
- **The spike is the tell.** Both Google and Yelp now treat a sudden surge of reviews on one profile as an event in itself. Google removes the content, pauses new reviews on the profile, emails the owner and shows readers a banner saying why. Yelp placed over 1,450 "media attention" alerts in 2025 that freeze posting when a business goes viral. An agent that posts reviews on a schedule produces exactly the shape these systems are built to stop, and the public banner is a reputational cost to the restaurant that a quiet filter is not.

## How it is detected

In the order a defender, a buyer or a regulator meets it.

1. **Velocity and age on the account, before the words.** The first screen at every platform named here is behavioural: how old the account is, how many reviews it has left and over what period, whether it has a purchase, booking or visit the platform can see, whether its sign-ins share a device, network or payment instrument with other reviewers of the same business. A fresh agent-run account fails most of these at once. Amazon's own argument against outside auditors is that they "make big assumptions without access to data signals showing patterns of abuse", which is a vendor defending itself but also a true statement about where the evidence is.
2. **The graph.** Amazon's graph neural networks and Yelp's ring investigations look for the same thing: a set of accounts that review the same businesses in the same windows. Yelp's 2025 numbers show the shift from flagging pages to closing people: suspicious-activity alerts on business pages fell 62% while account closures for ring activity rose 49% to 1,991, and it sent 1,020 reports to other platforms about groups trading reviews, 60% of which led to action. A ring is detectable across platforms because the same operators use the same accounts everywhere.
3. **The text, as a prior.** Classifiers for AI prose exist and Yelp runs one, but a defender should weight it low: it catches the lazy case and flags honest customers who used a tool. What travels better than style is repetition: the same phrasing, the same structure, the same product name position across reviews of one business, which CMA208 lists under "repetitive content" and Google under multiple accounts by one person.
4. **The business's own shape.** CMA208 chapter 8 is the audit script. Does the publisher have a policy, a risk assessment, a detection process, sanctions and a review cycle, each with evidence? A restaurant app whose feature list includes "agents post reviews" fails the first question in writing, which is the easiest kind of evidence a regulator gets. The CMA's enforcement so far has been warning letters to over 50 firms, undertakings from Google (January 2025) and Amazon on their detection systems, and on 27 March 2026 the first five formal investigations (Autotrader, Feefo, Dignity, Just Eat, Pasta Evangelists), two of them platforms and three of them traders.
5. **Provenance on the clip, not the review.** Where the marketing posts are AI video, the detection route is the one in last week's note: a C2PA manifest check on the file and the promoter's incentive, not the look of the clip.

What does not work: judging a single review by eye or by an AI-text score alone; star averages; and the assumption that a platform's silence means the reviews passed. What works: account age and velocity, the graph across accounts and platforms, a spike on one profile, and the paper trail the DMCC Act now obliges every publisher to keep.

## What I did not run, and why

The one measurement that would settle how good the screens are, posting a known-AI review and timing its removal, is the abuse itself, so it is never run, by me or in the sandbox. I did not make an account anywhere, did not touch Lovable or Higgsfield, and did not read the CMA's own enforcement pages at source for the 2026 investigations or the Euro Car Parks fine, so those two facts rest on three law-firm summaries that agree with each other. The 10% of turnover figure is from memory of the Act's direct-enforcement regime and is marked as such above.
