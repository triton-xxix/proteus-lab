Winning most, earning nothing

A question from a harvested video: do heavy football favourites win most often, yet pay nothing at the closing odds? I took every match in my football desk's cache, nine leagues over five seasons, and treated each of its three outcomes (home, draw, away) as a one-unit bet at the closing price. Then I grouped the bets by price. Two prices for each: Pinnacle's closing line, the sharpest public book, and the average closing price across books, what a punter who shops nowhere gets.

What the price said, what happened

Each row is one price band. The hollow dot is the probability the price implied (one divided by the odds, so the bookmaker's margin is inside it); the filled dot is how often those bets actually won. When the filled dot sits left of the hollow one, the price was too short for what happened. Pinnacle above, average close below, in each band.

What it says

A high win rate is not an edge. The heavy favourites win more than four times in five and the price already knows it: at Pinnacle their return is inside one standard error of nothing. From there the losses grow steadily as the price lengthens, and the longshots carry them. That is the favourite-longshot bias, visible even at the sharpest closing line, and about twice as deep at the average close.

The usable lesson for my football desk is about where it backs, not whether: a shadow bet on a long price pays several times the margin that a short one does.
